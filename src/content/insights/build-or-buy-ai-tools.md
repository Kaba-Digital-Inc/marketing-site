"Should we build this or buy it?" is the most expensive question in AI projects, because both wrong answers cost real money. Build when you should have bought and you pay for software you now have to maintain. Buy when you should have built and you fight a tool that never quite fits.

Our default is to **buy and configure first**. Custom engineering has to earn its place. Here is how we decide.

## Start with what you already pay for

Many businesses already own capable tools: the AI features in their email, accounting, CRM, helpdesk, and document software. Before adding anything, list what is already available and switched off or unused. Configuring and connecting existing tools is the cheapest option and the quickest to reverse.

## Questions that settle it

**1. Does a tool already fit the process as it actually runs?** Test it on real examples, not on the vendor demo. If it handles most cases and fails gracefully on the rest, buy it.

**2. Is this workflow part of how you win?** If a process is routine for every company in your industry, buy. If it is a real source of advantage, such as your pricing logic, your matching of customers to services, or your service quality, custom work may be justified.

**3. How sensitive is the data, and where must it live?** Some tools cannot meet your privacy, residency, or contractual requirements. When control over data is non-negotiable, custom or self-hosted options become more attractive.

**4. How many systems does it have to connect?** A tool that works alone is easy. A workflow that crosses five systems, each with its own quirks, is often where light custom integration pays off.

**5. Who will run it in a year?** Every system needs an owner. If nobody on your team can maintain a custom build, you are signing up for a dependency on whoever wrote it.

## A simple rule of thumb

| Situation | Usual answer |
| --- | --- |
| Common process, a good tool exists | Buy and configure |
| Common process, tool is close but needs glue | Buy, then add a small integration |
| Process is a competitive advantage | Consider building |
| Strict data control requirements | Evaluate self-hosted or custom |
| Nobody to maintain it | Buy, or budget for managed support |

## The hybrid is usually right

Most successful setups are a mix: a purchased tool for the core job, a thin layer of custom automation connecting it to your other systems, and a human approval step where the stakes are higher. That keeps the custom part small, which keeps it cheap to run and easy to replace.

## What to watch for in vendors

- **Data handling:** what is stored, for how long, and whether your data is used to train their models
- **Export and exit:** whether you can take your data and configuration with you
- **Pricing that scales with use:** usage-based costs can surprise you once a workflow succeeds
- **Accuracy claims:** ask how the vendor measures quality and test it on your own examples
